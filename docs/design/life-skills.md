## 0. The short version

- **Two pillars, one game.** The School teaches the subjects. **Life** teaches how to live: staying healthy and safe,
  handling feelings, getting on with people, money, home and everyday jobs, getting around, life online, thinking well,
  caring for the world, and the future (jobs, plans).
- **Learn it by doing it.** Every life skill is an **Experience**: the child does the real steps with their hands (in VR)
  or their fingers (on a phone), in a calm, safe place, again and again until it is easy. A short card then names what
  they did, and the School's quick memory brings it back later.
- **Take it home.** Every skill ends with a **Try it for real** card for the child and a grown-up ("Find your smoke
  alarm together and press its test button"). Real life is the goal, not the game.
- **Safe and kind.** Emergencies are practice, never scary: a calm coach, no injuries shown, no shame, always a way to
  finish. Real procedures from real authorities, with the sources written down. Country-aware where it matters (the
  emergency number, which side cars drive on).
- **Free forever.** Safety and health knowledge is never behind a payment.
- **Easy means obvious (#113).** A child understands every Experience without reading or being told: the coach
  shows with ghost hands, the next thing glows, one thing at a time, everything read aloud. If a child is stuck, that is
  our bug (§10).
- **Parents see the learning (#113).** A quick "what do you know?" before and after each Experience, a weekly "your
  child can now..." summary, and the child *showing* the grown-up at home (§11).
- **A platform, not only a game (#113).** Later (#114: "the future, not now"), a **Classroom** mode for a teacher with
  up to 20-30 headsets: one lesson on every headset, the teacher sees everyone's progress, pauses all (§12).
- **One method for every real skill (#116, #117).** A real place, the real tools and devices used the way they really
  are, the reason for each step, safety first, practice until easy, then try it for real. One engine: VR-HANDS' grab, ten
  tool motions (strike, stroke, twist, squeeze, press, spread, measure, pour, aim, cut) and one device model (§13).
- **Dangerous things, learned safely (#118).** What hurts children most (roads, water, fire, falls, poisons) practised
  here, many times, with **safe mistakes**: the wrong way tried, time frozen, the coach's calm why, then the right way
  (§14).
- **First wave (L1):** the Life tab, the Life Passport, the grown-ups' page, and three flagship Experiences: **Little
  Lifesaver** (first aid and calling 112), **Fire Escape**, **My Money Stall**. Then the street with the new cars
  (#109), the kitchen and the calm corner, then the rest.

## 1. Why a game, and why VR

What the evidence says (sources in §15):
- **Doing beats reading for skills.** A 2026 umbrella review of 93 studies found VR often improves motor, procedural and
  safety skills [L1]. A meta-analysis of 54 studies of virtual experiments found that the more of the body the learner
  uses (whole-body and hands-on interaction), the bigger the effect [L2]. In a VR lesson, how well students did in the
  virtual task predicted how well they did the real one [L2].
- **Practice needs repeats.** In randomized trials, VR road-crossing training made 7- and 8-year-olds cross more safely,
  but it took several sessions, and VR practice changed behaviour more than knowledge [L3]. So: repeated short practice
  for the behaviour, plus cards for the knowledge, plus a real walk with a grown-up.
- **Feeling it matters.** In PwC's study of soft-skills training (adults), VR learners felt 3.75 times more emotionally
  connected to the material and 2.75 times more confident to act on it than classroom learners [L4]. Self-reported, but
  it fits what children tell us about the game: they remember what they *did*.
- **Some things should be learned before they are needed.** The WHO-endorsed "Kids Save Lives" statement asks for two
  hours of CPR training a year for every schoolchild from about 12 [L5]. A child cannot practise a fire or an
  emergency call for real; a game can let them practise safely, many times.
- **But children are not small adults.** Young children can form false memories from VR (Segovia and Bailenson
  2009) [L6]. Stanford and Common Sense Media advise short sessions (5-10 minutes for young children, about 20 for
  older ones) and supervision [L7]. Children 6-8 fear monsters and the dark; older children fear realistic dangers more
  (our psychology rules §1.11). So emergencies must be calm, cartoon-gentle and clearly "practice".

## 2. What "everything they need in life" means: the Life map

Built on the WHO's ten core life skills (self-awareness, empathy, critical thinking, creative thinking,
decision-making, problem-solving, communication, relationships, coping with stress, coping with emotions) [L8], CASEL's
five social and emotional competencies (self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills,
responsible decision-making) [L9], UNICEF's twelve core life skills in four dimensions (learning to know, to do, to be,
to live together) [L10], the OECD/INFE and EU financial competence frameworks (money and transactions, planning and
managing, risk and reward, the financial landscape; plus digital finance, sustainability and entrepreneurship) [L11],
and practical safety from the authorities that teach it (the Red Cross and Red Crescent, the European Resuscitation
Council, the WHO, national emergency services).

Ten areas. Each skill has three tracks, **Little** (about 6-8), **Middle** (about 9-11) and **Big** (12 and up). The
game cannot tell ages apart (our age band is only under or over 13), so the child or a grown-up picks the track, and a

## 3. How the game teaches a life skill: the Experience loop

1. **A reason.** A story moment, never a lecture: a buddy burnt its paw on the stove; the market opens tomorrow and your
   stall is empty; the smoke alarm beeps in the practice house.
2. **Do it.** The child does the real steps in the right order, with hands in VR (grab, push, pour, turn, crouch, look
   both ways) or taps on a phone. A calm **coach** shows the step once if asked, and only if asked.
3. **Feedback, kindly.** What went well and what to change, about the action, never the child ("You looked both ways:
   that's how you stay safe" — effort and strategy, the psychology rules §1.8). Nothing is "failed"; a try that missed a
   step shows that step and goes again.
4. **Name it.** A short card (read aloud) with the 3-5 key steps, the same words the real authorities use ("Get down,
   get out, stay out, call 112").
5. **Again, later.** The skill comes back days later, a little different (another room on fire, another street, a
   new customer at the stall): spaced practice, because one go is not enough [L3].
6. **Try it for real.** A **Try it for real** card: something to do with a grown-up at home or outside, never inside
   the game. The grown-ups' page lists them.
7. **Mastery.** Each skill has three stamps in the **Life Passport**: *Done it* (with the coach's help), *By myself* (no
   hints), *Expert* (a harder version: a different room, less time, a new twist). Stamps are informational, not paid:
   they show what the child can do.

## 4. VR: what only a headset can do (and the phone keeps up)

Every Experience works on a phone, the web and a PC; VR makes the hands and the body part of it:
- **Hands do the steps:** two hands on a training dummy's chest, pushing to the beat of a song (100-120 a minute, the
  controllers buzz on each good push); holding a cloth on a cut; turning a tap; pouring and measuring; using a
  screwdriver; giving change coin by coin.
- **The body does the steps:** really getting low under smoke (the headset's height), really turning the head left and
  right at the kerb (the game checks the look), reaching, crouching, standing still and calm.
- **Feeling of being there:** standing in your own kitchen-sized room, on a real-sized street with real-sized cars,
  in front of a friendly audience. That is what makes it stick [L1, L2, L4].
- **Comfort and care:** sessions short by design (an Experience takes 5-10 minutes; the 30-minute break card stays), a
  calm place to stand, no sudden scares, the fade instead of camera jumps, seated options; and the VR rules of
  `vr-research.md`. Meta's Quest accounts are for 10 and up [L12]: younger children use the flat versions.

## 6. Careful topics, safety and the law

- **Real procedures, real sources.** Every step comes from the authority that teaches it (the IFRC and the national Red
  Cross or Red Crescent, the ERC, the WHO, the fire service, the road safety authorities), with the source written
  beside it in the notes, like the School's facts. Each first aid Experience says "This is practice. A real first aid
  course teaches more" and points to one.
- **Country-aware:** the emergency number (112 in Georgia since 2012 [L13] and across Europe; 911 in the US and Canada;
  999 in the UK...) and which side cars drive on (so "look left, right, left" or "right, left, right") come from a
  **region** the grown-up sets on the grown-ups' page (default from the device's region).
- **Calm, never scary:** no blood, no injuries shown, no screaming, no fire that fills the view; smoke is a soft grey;
  the person who needs help is a friendly buddy or a dummy; the coach is always there; every Experience can stop at any
  moment ("I need a break") and always ends well. Little-track emergencies are the gentlest. After an emergency
  Experience, a short calm debrief ("You did it. In real life, grown-ups help too").
- **Framed as practice** (the false-memory finding [L6]): the practice house, the training dummy, the coach's
  whistle: it is clear the child is practising, not living through an emergency.
- **Nothing personal is kept or sent.** The address and phone number game is said aloud to a grown-up; the game never
  asks the child to type them, never stores them, never sends them. No microphone recording (Speak Up uses timing and
  looking, not the voice). The Life progress is part of the existing profile backup and nothing more.
- **Topics that need a grown-up and an expert:** body safety, puberty, consent, mental-health crises, alcohol and
  drugs. Not in the first waves. When they come, they are written with a child psychologist and a teacher, behind the
  grown-ups' choice (off until a grown-up turns them on), never in multiplayer, and they always point to a trusted adult
  and the country's child helpline.
- **An expert review before each flagship ships:** a first aid trainer for #1, a fire safety officer for #2 (the owner
  arranges; the lead prepares the review sheet with every step and its source).
- **Never paid.** Safety, health and money basics are free in every version; paid universes and looks stay the paid
  side (the forever roadmap §4).

## 10. Easy means obvious: how a child understands without being told (#113)

EasyCraft's name is the rule. Every Experience is built so a 6-year-old who cannot read gets it right the first time:
- **Show, don't tell.** The coach does the step once with see-through "ghost hands" (VR) or a ghost finger (phone); the
  child copies. No paragraph of instructions anywhere.
- **The next thing glows.** Exactly one thing to do at a time, softly lit; everything else waits.
- **Heard, not read.** Every line is read aloud for the Little track (ReadAloud), short, with a picture.
- **Same hands everywhere.** Grip takes, trigger uses, the wrist opens the menu, A says yes: the same in every
  Experience, taught once in a two-minute "hands" warm-up (pop the bubbles, catch the ball, press the big button).
- **Big, forgiving, undoable.** Big targets, generous hit areas, a step can be redone, nothing is lost.
- **Never stuck.** No progress for about 10 seconds: the coach shows the step again (by doing, not by text).
- **The whole game, not only Life.** The same bar applies to every screen and every first-time moment (starting, building,
  the School, the rocket, VR): an **ease audit** (E0 in the plan) walks each one as a child who cannot read would, and
  fixes what needs words to understand.
- **Measured.** Each Experience logs (on the device) how often a first-time child does each step without the hint and how
  long it takes; a step most children miss gets redesigned. The owner's own children and the first classroom are the
  playtests (`docs/playtests/`).

## 11. Parents see how fast their child learns (#113)

- **Before and after.** A 30-second picture check before an Experience ("What do you do first if you smell smoke?") and
  the same after; the grown-ups' page shows the change ("Fire Escape: knew 1 of 5 steps, now 5 of 5, in 9 minutes").
- **"Your child can now..."** A weekly summary on the grown-ups' page in plain words: "call 112 and say where they are",
  "get out of a smoky room safely", "give change from 10", with the Passport's stamps.
- **The child teaches the grown-up.** Each **Try it for real** card asks the child to *show* a grown-up (teaching
  someone else is one of the strongest ways to learn, and it is what makes a parent excited).
- **Honest numbers.** Only what the game measured, never made-up praise; nothing leaves the device but the existing
  backup.

## 12. A platform: EasyCraft Classroom (#113: "maybe later i will buy 20 vr")

The owner may set up lessons with about 20 headsets. The platform is designed for it now and built after L1:
- **The teacher's console:** the same game on a tablet or PC in **Teacher** mode, running the class on the room's Wi-Fi
  (no internet needed; the classroom's own server on the teacher's device, or the class on our server with a class code).
- **Children join without accounts:** a class code and a picture name (a fox, a star...) plus a seat number; no real
  names, no personal data. Each headset plays the Experience on its own (smooth, nothing to sync); groups of 2-4 join
  for Team Challenges.
- **The teacher's controls:** start a lesson on every headset, see each child's step and stamps live, a raised-hand
  signal from a child who needs help, **Pause all** (every view fades to a calm room with the teacher's words), bring
  everyone back, end the lesson; a timer set to safe session lengths (about 20 minutes for 10+ [L7]).
- **Lesson plans:** for each Experience, a one-page plan: the goal, a 5-minute talk before, the 15-20 minutes in the
  headset, a 5-minute talk after, the questions to ask, the **Try it for real** homework.
- **The class report:** who has which stamps, which steps the class found hard, before-and-after numbers; kept on the
  teacher's device (or the school's choice).
- **The headsets:** Meta's own education service (Horizon managed services with Shared Mode) stops being sold from 20
  February 2026 [L14], so the class runs our app in a single-app ("kiosk") setup through a third-party device manager
  (for example ManageXR or ArborXR [L15]) or plain developer installs; Quest accounts are 10+ (§4), so younger classes use
  tablets. Seated or standing spots with their own guardian boundary, face covers, staff in the room.
- **Size:** L (a phase of its own, C1: the future, when the owner says; #114).

## 13. Learn to use any tool and any device: the method for every real skill (#116, #117)

> The owner (2026-10-16 15:04, #116): "using vr it should be possible to build a house for example like in real world,
> take brick or stones and put in apropriate place and etc like in real life"; (15:10, #117): "building is one of the
> example how we are going to teach childrens, also like in development there should be different instruments used while
> building houses, we should teach and they should be able to use any instrument and devices. but this is one example, we
> must use same aproach for any skills children need in real life"

**The method.** Every real skill is learned the same way: **a real place** (a building site, a kitchen, a workshop, a
clinic, a shop, a garden), **the real tools and devices** used the way they are really used (the motion, the grip, the
order of steps), **the reason** behind each step (why bricks are staggered, why you unplug before you clean), **safety
first** (goggles, gloves, unplugged, hot things, sharp things), **practice until it is easy** (the stamps), and **try it
for real** with a grown-up. One engine, many skills: a new skill is a new place, its tools and its steps, never new code
for how hands work.

**Tools: a hand's motion, recognised.** Every tool is held (VR-HANDS' shared grab: a `VrGrabbable` of kind `&"tool"`) and
used with one of a small set of **real motions**, recognised from the hand in VR and from a simple gesture on a phone:

| Motion | Real tools | In VR | On a phone |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strike** | hammer, mallet, nail punch, drum | a swing that lands on the target, its speed checked | tap on the target |
| **Stroke** | saw, file, sandpaper, paint roller, brush, broom, iron | back and forth along a line | drag back and forth |
| **Twist** | screwdriver, wrench, key, tap, jar lid, dial | the wrist turns while the tool touches | a circle drawn |
| **Squeeze** | pliers, extinguisher, glue gun, spray, stapler, oven glove | the trigger held, pressure in the trigger's pull | press and hold |
| **Press** | drill, buttons, a stamp, CPR, a switch | pushed into the target, depth and rate | press and hold, or tap to a beat |
| **Spread** | trowel, knife (butter), spatula, plaster | a sweeping pass over a surface | drag over the surface |
| **Measure** | tape, ruler, spirit level, scales, thermometer, stopwatch, protractor | stretched between two points, held against a surface | drag from start to end |
| **Pour** | jug, watering can, measuring cup, kettle | tilted over the target, the amount by the tilt and time | hold and tilt |
| **Aim** | spirit level, plumb line, camera, telescope, laser measure | held still and pointed | point and hold |
| **Cut** | scissors, knife (with a child-safe grip), secateurs | along a line with the claw grip shown | drag along the line |

Each tool says: its name, what it is for, how to hold it, its motion, its safety rule, and **the right tool for the
job** (using a screwdriver as a hammer is noticed, kindly: "That works better with a hammer"). A tool used well buzzes
softly in the hand (Vr.haptic); a slip is shown, never punished.

**Devices: buttons, dials, displays and an order of steps.** Machines and appliances are one model: **controls** (buttons,
dials, switches, a keypad, a door, a lid), **what it shows** (a display, a light, a sound), and **the right order** with
its safety steps: a microwave (what may go in, the door, the time, start), an oven and a hob (handles in, oven gloves), a
kettle (the fill line, off before pouring), a washing machine (sort, load, the powder, the programme), a vacuum, a drill
(the chuck, the speed, goggles), a sewing machine, a phone (call 112: `life-skills.md` §5), a computer and a printer, a
camera, a bank's cash machine and a card reader (pretend; never a real PIN), a bike's gears and brakes, a car's dashboard
(the cars, #109), a microscope and lab scales (the Lab), a fuse box (look, don't touch: a grown-up's job).

**Built once, used everywhere.** The engine has three parts, all shared: VR-HANDS' grab (taking and holding), **the tool
motions** (one recogniser for the ten motions, VR and flat, with each motion's checks: speed, path, angle, depth, rate,
time) and **the device model** (controls, displays, the order of steps, the safety steps). Every Life Experience, the Lab,
the School's practical rounds, Real Builder and the workshop use the same three, so a child who learns the screwdriver in
the workshop already knows it in the kitchen drawer, and a new skill costs a place and its steps, not new hand code.

**The Tool Book.** Like the Secret Book and the Passport: every tool and device a child has used, its picture, what it is
for, its safety rule and its three stamps (Done it, By myself, Expert). "You can use 23 tools" is something a parent sees
(§11).

**Safety, as everywhere (§6):** sharp, hot and powered tools come with their protective step first (goggles, gloves,
unplug, oven gloves, the child-safe grip); some are "a grown-up's job" in real life and the game says so (the fuse box,
the chainsaw, the iron on the Little track); the Try it for real cards only ask for what a child may do with a grown-up.

**The plan:** the tool motions and the device model are frozen as a contract by the lead (like the grab) when VR-HANDS'
grab lands; **round 4b** builds them with **Real Builder** as their first user; every later Life Experience (L2-L4) and
the Lab's VR bench use them; the catalogue grows area by area.

## 14. Dangerous things, learned safely (#118)

> The owner (2026-10-16 19:51, #118): "consider somethimes in real life teaching some staff is dangerous, we can use this
> online environment to teach them safely, so think what such things we can implement into game"

**Why this matters most.** The things that kill and hurt children are the things nobody can let them practise: the WHO and
UNICEF's world report on child injury puts **road traffic, drowning, burns, falls and poisoning** first (about 260,000,
175,000, 96,000, 47,000 and 45,000 children's deaths a year), and for the youngest, choking [L16]. A game can let a child
practise exactly these, many times, with nothing at stake. Knowledge alone is not enough (a dog-bite prevention program
taught knowledge but not safer behaviour [L17]); practice is.

**Safe mistakes: the game's own superpower.** In real life a mistake with fire or water can't be allowed; here it can, and
it teaches: training that lets learners make errors and learn from them transfers better to real situations than training
that avoids errors (a meta-analysis of 24 studies: d = 0.56 after training, d = 0.80 for new kinds of situation [L18]).
So on the Middle and Big tracks a step may let the child **try the wrong way**: water on a pan fire, stepping out between
parked cars, grabbing the person stuck to a cable. At that moment **time freezes**, the scene goes soft and still, and the
coach shows calmly what would happen and why ("Water makes burning oil jump up. That's why we put a lid on it."), then the
child does it right. Never gory, never sudden, never shaming; on the Little track the coach guides before any mistake.
**Time slows at danger moments** on early tries and runs at real speed for the Expert stamp.

**The rules for every dangerous Experience** (with §6):
- **Prevention first, then what to do:** spot the danger, keep away, then the response.
- **The authority's exact steps**, with sources, and the expert's review before it ships (the fire service, the national
  Red Cross or Red Crescent, the WHO, poison centres, road safety and water safety bodies, electricity safety bodies).
- **"Not for a child to do" is said plainly:** power lines, mixing chemicals, going back into a fire, swimming out to
  save someone: avoid it, and call for help (the region's number).
- **A calm intensity setting** on the grown-ups' page (gentle by default; a grown-up may allow "more real" on the Big
  track), the debrief after every emergency Experience, and the way out at any moment.
- **Never in the game:** handling weapons, making anything dangerous (no recipes, ever), using alcohol or drugs (only
  saying no and getting help), self-harm (only the helplines), risky stunts.
