## The facts and their sources
- Water ice in the Moon's permanently shadowed craters: in October 2009 a spacecraft (LCROSS) crashed its rocket stage
  into Cabeus crater near the south pole and saw water in the plume. NASA, *LCROSS Impact Data Indicates Water on Moon*
  (13 Nov 2009); Colaprete et al. (2010), *Science* 330. Surface ice seen directly: Li et al. (2018), *PNAS* 115.
- The Moon's axis is tilted only about 1.5°, so near the poles the Sun stays low and deep crater floors are always in
  shadow; they are among the coldest places measured in the solar system (below −230 °C; LRO Diviner). The sunlit ground
  reaches about 120 °C. NASA, *Moon: Poles*; Paige et al. (2010), *Science* 330.
- Water is H₂O; electrolysis gives twice as much hydrogen as oxygen by volume, and 9 kg of water hold 1 kg of hydrogen and
  8 kg of oxygen (by mass). Liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen are rocket fuel. The space station makes its oxygen by
  electrolysis. NASA, *Oxygen Generation System*.
- The seas (maria) are basalt lava that flooded big basins about 3-3.9 billion years ago; early astronomers named them
  seas. They hold ilmenite (FeTiO₃: iron, titanium, oxygen); some sea basalts are rich in titanium. NASA, *Moon: Maria*;
  USGS.
- Moon rock and dust are about 40-45% oxygen by weight; heating ilmenite with hydrogen gives water (then oxygen), and
  molten-regolith electrolysis pulls oxygen straight out. NASA, *Oxygen from regolith*; ESA, *Moon dust into oxygen*
  (2020).
- Titanium is about as strong as steel and much lighter: planes and spacecraft use it. Britannica, *Titanium*.
- Helium-3: the solar wind implants it in the Moon's dust (Earth's magnetic field and air turn the wind away); the richest
  titanium-rich mature dust holds about 10-20 parts per billion (about 20 mg a tonne); heating to about 600-700 °C
  releases most of it. It is used in dilution refrigerators and neutron detectors; fusion power with it is only an idea.
  Wittenberg et al. (1986), *Fusion Technology*; Fa & Jin (2007), *Icarus* 190; US DOE, *Helium-3 supply*.
- KREEP is named for potassium (K), rare earth elements (REE) and phosphorus (P); it is concentrated round Oceanus
  Procellarum and Imbrium; its thorium was mapped from orbit by a gamma-ray spectrometer (Lunar Prospector, 1998): KREEP
  terrain counts several to ten times the highlands'. Lawrence et al. (1998), *Science* 281; Britannica, *KREEP*.
- Rare earths (neodymium, dysprosium) make the strongest permanent magnets: electric motors, wind turbines, headphones.
  USGS, *Rare Earth Elements* (Fact Sheet 2014-3078).
- Meteoroids hit the Moon at about 20 km/s on average; with no air, nothing slows them, and without weather its craters
  last. Most shooting stars burn up in Earth's air. Iron meteorites come from the cores of broken, once-molten asteroids
  and are mostly iron with 5-30% nickel; pieces of meteorites were found in Apollo soil. NASA, *Meteors & Meteorites*;
  Joy et al. (2012), *Science* 336.
- Mars's shallow ground ice: in 2008 the Phoenix lander dug a trench and saw white ice that vanished (sublimated) within
  days; fresh impact craters seen from orbit (HiRISE) exposed bright ice in the mid-latitudes. NASA/JPL, *Phoenix: Bright
  chunks* (2008); Byrne et al. (2009), *Science* 325. Ice under dust stays; in sunshine it turns straight to gas.
- Mars's polar caps hold enough water ice to cover the planet about 20 m deep (the south polar layered deposits alone
  ~11 m). Plaut et al. (2007), *Science* 316; NASA.
- Plans to make rocket fuel on Mars mix carbon dioxide from the air with hydrogen (methane, the Sabatier process) and keep
  the oxygen. NASA, *Mars Design Reference Architecture 5.0*.
- An astronaut drinks about 2-3 litres of water a day. NASA, *Water on the Space Station*.
- Hematite "blueberries" (concretions, mostly under 6 mm, about 4 mm) found by Opportunity at Meridiani Planum in 2004
  grew in water-soaked rock; hematite had been seen there from orbit. Squyres et al. (2004), *Science* 306; NASA/JPL.
  Hematite is a main iron ore on Earth. USGS, *Iron Ore*.
- Mars is red because its dust holds iron oxide (rust). Magnets on the Mars rovers collected airborne dust: nearly all of
  it is magnetic. Madsen et al. (2009), *JGR* 114. Curiosity studied the active Bagnold dunes in December 2015 (the first
  close look at moving dunes on another planet). NASA/JPL. Planet-wide dust storms happen (2018). NASA.
- Phoenix found perchlorate (ClO₄⁻) in the soil (about 0.5%); it is not good for people (the thyroid), so soil would be
  washed before growing food; its brines stay liquid far below 0 °C; heated, perchlorate gives off oxygen, and the
  space station's backup oxygen candles use a perchlorate/chlorate salt. Hecht et al. (2009), *Science* 325; NASA.
- Chloride salt deposits lie in low basins of Mars's southern highlands (old lakes that dried). Osterloo et al. (2008),
  *Science* 319.
- In January 2005 Opportunity found Heat Shield Rock, a basketball-sized iron-nickel meteorite: the first meteorite found
  on another planet. NASA/JPL. Mars's thin air slows small meteoroids, and its dry cold keeps them for a long time.
- A solar furnace's mirrors reach more than 3,000 °C (Odeillo, France: 3,500 °C); concentrated sunlight has sintered
  Moon-dust simulant into bricks. ESA, *3D printing bricks from moondust using the power of the Sun* (2017).
- Oxygen from the Moon's rock, water and fuel from its ice, bricks from its dust: "in-situ resource utilization". NASA,
  *ISRU*.
