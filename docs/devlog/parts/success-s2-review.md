## The Goal Board

For the review by a child psychologist or school counsellor (goals, if-then plans, habits).

The device **calendar**: controls: the day squares. It shows: the days kept; today. The right order: choose one small habit → do it → tick today → a day off: just carry on tomorrow. Safety: no guilt: a missed day never undoes the others.
The device **jar**: controls: the slot; the lid. It shows: how much is saved; the goal's line. The right order: save first, after each lesson → count it each week → spend it on the goal. Safety: keep real money at home; count real money with a grown-up.

The "name it" card: **One step at a time**

1. A goal is something you can do and see.
2. Big jobs: one step at a time.
3. If it falls down, try again.
4. Save now for something better later.
5. A small habit every day: a missed day is fine.

Its source line: The Dunedin study of self-control (Moffitt et al., 2011) and research on how habits form (Lally et al., 2010)

Try it for real: With a grown-up: choose one small goal for this week, like a tower of 10 blocks or laying the table, and plan its first step.

Your child can now: pick a goal they can do, build it one step at a time, try again after a fall and save for something bigger

Sources:

- Self-control in childhood predicts health, wealth and staying out of trouble as adults, and it can be learned. (Moffitt et al. (2011), PNAS 108(7), the Dunedin study): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3041102
- A new daily habit took from 18 to 254 days to become automatic (66 in the middle); missing one day did not materially affect it. (Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts and Wardle (2010), European Journal of Social Psychology 40(6)): https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674
- Waiting for something better and finishing what you start are self-mastery skills for young children. (EntreComp (2016): motivation and perseverance): https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC101581

### Florence Nightingale (1820-1910), the United Kingdom: The nurse with the lamp

1. In 1854, Florence Nightingale went to look after soldiers in a war hospital far from home.
2. The hospital was dirty and crowded. Many soldiers got ill from germs, not from the fighting.
3. Florence and her nurses cleaned, washed, cooked good food and let in fresh air.
4. At night she walked round the wards with a lamp, checking on the soldiers.
5. She also counted. Her charts showed that most of that illness could have been stopped.
6. In 1860 she started a school for nurses in London, and nursing became a job you train for.

Question: "Florence's charts showed people what was really making soldiers ill. Which skill did she use?" **Using numbers to show the truth** / Being lucky / Shouting louder

### Mary Seacole (1805-1881), Jamaica: Mother Seacole's hotel

1. Mary Seacole grew up in Jamaica and learned how to care for sick people from her mother.
2. When a war began far away, she asked to go and help the soldiers. She was turned down.

### Louis Braille (1809-1852), France: Letters you can feel

1. Louis Braille lost his sight after an accident when he was three.
2. At his school for blind children in Paris, the few books had huge raised letters, slow to feel.

Question: "Hassan built with cheap mud bricks instead of costly concrete. Which skill did he use?" **Using what you have, cleverly** / Buying the dearest things / Giving up when it went wrong

### Carlos Finlay (1833-1915), Cuba: Nobody believed him, at first

1. Carlos Finlay was a doctor in Cuba. A disease called yellow fever made many people very ill there.
2. In 1881 he said: mosquitoes carry the disease from one person to another.
3. Many doctors laughed at the idea. For about 20 years, few believed him.
4. Carlos kept studying and explaining.
5. In 1900 a team led by Walter Reed tested his idea and found he was right. Reed said the discovery was Finlay's.
6. Getting rid of the water where mosquitoes breed helped stop yellow fever.

Question: "For about 20 years few believed Carlos, but he kept on. Which skill did he use?" **Keeping going with the evidence** / Changing his mind to fit in / Getting cross with everyone

Sources:

- On 14 August 1881 Finlay proposed in Havana that mosquitoes carry yellow fever; the idea met ridicule; Walter Reed's commission confirmed it in 1900. https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/fever-yellow-fever-and-scientific-method/
- Reed's commission tested the mosquito idea in Cuba in 1900; mosquito control followed. https://www.britannica.com/biography/Walter-Reed

### Marie Curie (1867-1934), Poland and France: Two Nobel Prizes

1. Marie Sklodowska grew up in Poland, where girls could not go to university. She saved up and went to Paris to study.
2. With her husband Pierre, she worked for years in a cold, leaky shed, studying rays from strange rocks.
3. They found two new elements, polonium and radium.
4. In 1903 she became the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, and in 1911 she won a second one.
5. She is the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.
6. In the First World War she set up X-ray cars to help doctors look after wounded soldiers.

Question: "Marie worked for years in a cold shed to find radium. Which skill did she use?" **Patience and hard work** / Quick luck / Stopping when it got hard

Sources:

- She shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics, the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, and won the 1911 Prize in Chemistry for polonium and radium. https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1911/marie-curie/facts/
- The only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences; in the First World War she equipped vehicles with X-ray machines for the front. https://www.lshtm.ac.uk/aboutus/introducing/history/frieze/marie-sklodowska-curie
